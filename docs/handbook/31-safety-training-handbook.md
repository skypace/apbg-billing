## Part A — Our safety program

### A1. Our commitments and your rights

You have the legal right to:

- A safe workplace (Cal/OSHA, Title 8 CCR; federal OSHA Act §5(a)(1)).
- See the **Safety Data Sheet** for any chemical you work with, any time, without asking permission
  — our library is digital and searchable (§B9).
- **Report a hazard, injury, or violation without retaliation** — to your supervisor, the EH&S
  Lead, or Cal/OSHA directly at **1-833-579-0927**. Retaliation against you for that is illegal and
  is a firing offense here.
- **Refuse work you reasonably believe is imminently dangerous** while it gets evaluated. You will
  not lose pay for exercising this.
- See your own exposure and medical records (8 CCR §3204).
- Have an employee representative present during a Cal/OSHA inspection.

We commit to: fixing reported hazards on a schedule and telling you what we did, providing all
required PPE at no cost to you, training you *before* we assign you a task with a hazard in it,
never asking you to hide anything from an inspector, and never pressuring you to beat a schedule
by cutting a safety step.

### A2. How the program runs (our IIPP)

Cal/OSHA §3203 requires every California employer to have a written Injury and Illness Prevention
Program. Ours works like this:

| Element | How we do it |
|---|---|
| **Who's responsible** | Sky Pace, CEO, is the EH&S Lead with authority and budget. Supervisors are responsible for their crews. You are responsible for your own work. |
| **Communication** | This handbook, tailgate/toolbox talks, the compliance app, and direct reporting to any supervisor. Anonymous reports are accepted and acted on. |
| **Hazard identification** | Scheduled walkthroughs of Hangar 200; pre-trip vehicle inspections; pre-operation forklift checks; job-site look-around at every customer account; plus any hazard you report. |
| **Investigation** | Every injury and every near-miss gets looked into — what happened, why, what change prevents a repeat. Not who to blame. |
| **Correction** | Unsafe conditions get fixed by severity: imminent danger stops work immediately; everything else gets an owner and a due date. |
| **Training** | This handbook + hands-on for your job + annual refreshers (§H1). |
| **Records** | Training signatures, inspection logs, injury records (Cal/OSHA Log 300), and corrective actions — kept in the compliance system. |

### A3. Safe-work expectations

Following this handbook is a condition of employment, the same as showing up on time. We enforce it
consistently — and that protects you, because a rule nobody enforces is a rule that gets someone
hurt eventually.

Things that will get you sent home or terminated, not coached:

- Operating a forklift, doing hot work, or handling refrigerant without current certification.
- Driving impaired, or using a handheld phone while driving.
- Removing or defeating a machine guard, interlock, or pressure-relief device.
- Bypassing lockout to save time.
- Hiding an injury, or pressuring a coworker not to report one.
- Concealing anything from a regulator or falsifying a record.

### A4. Stop-work authority

**Every employee has the authority to stop a job** — yours or anyone else's — when you believe
continuing will hurt someone. You do not need permission and you do not need to be right. Say "I'm
stopping this," make the area safe, and call your supervisor.

If a customer, a manager, or a schedule is pushing you to do something unsafe, the answer is: *"I
can't do it that way — let me call my supervisor and we'll figure out how to get it done."* Nobody
at Brix will ever back a customer over your safety. We have never lost an account over this.

## Part B — Core safety (everyone)

### B1. Personal protective equipment

We provide it free, we replace it on request, no questions asked. Damaged PPE gets replaced, not
taped.

| Task | Minimum PPE |
|---|---|
| Any chemical handling (cleaners, sanitizers, acids, cements) | Safety glasses + chemical-resistant gloves |
| Line cleaning / caustic chemicals | Safety glasses **and** face shield + chemical gloves + apron |
| CO₂ / nitrogen connect, disconnect, cylinder fill | Safety glasses + insulated gloves |
| Grinding, cutting, drilling, hammering | Safety glasses; face shield for overhead or heavy grinding |
| Brazing / hot work | Shaded eye protection, leather gloves, long sleeves, no synthetics |
| Handling sharp steel, dismantling, scrap | Cut-resistant gloves |
| Warehouse floor, forklift areas, loading | High-visibility vest, closed-toe non-slip footwear |
| Delivery routes | Closed-toe non-slip footwear, back-support optional, hi-vis when curbside |

**Footwear is on you but it is mandatory:** closed-toe, non-slip sole, in good condition. No
sneakers with worn tread on a wet kitchen floor. No sandals or clogs, ever, anywhere on the
warehouse floor or in a customer's kitchen.

**Jewelry:** no loose rings, bracelets, chains, or lanyards near rotating equipment, forklifts, or
when handling cylinders. Rings degloved fingers — that's the actual injury.

If a task seems to need PPE we don't stock, stop and ask before starting.

### B2. Lifting, carrying, and your back

![Correct lifting posture: back straight, knees bent, load close to the body — versus bending at the waist. Plus when to get a team lift.](/billing/safety/lifting.svg)

This is our most common injury and it's the one that follows you home for thirty years. A 5-gallon
BIB is 40–50 lb of shifting liquid. A 20 lb CO₂ cylinder is 50+ lb full. A full keg is 160 lb.

**How to lift:**

1. Size it up first. Test the weight before committing.
2. Feet shoulder-width, one slightly forward. Get close — the load rides against your body.
3. Bend your knees and hips, not your back. Keep the natural curve in your spine.
4. Lift smoothly with your legs. No jerking.
5. **Never twist while loaded.** Turn with your feet.
6. Set down the same way you picked up.

**Get help or gear when:**

- The load is over ~50 lb, or awkward, or you can't see over it.
- You're reaching above shoulder height or below your knees.
- The path has stairs, a ramp, a curb, or a doorway you have to hold open.
- You'd have to twist, or you're on a wet or uneven surface.

**A team lift is one person calling it.** "Lift on three — one, two, three." Same height, same pace,
same direction, and one person calls the set-down too.

**Never catch a falling load.** Boxes, cylinders, product — let it go. Steel and syrup are cheap;
your back and feet are not.

**Repetitive motion (8 CCR §5110):** route work is dozens of lifts a day. Vary your motion, use the
hand truck for anything you'd otherwise carry twice, and tell your supervisor about a developing
ache *before* it becomes an injury — early is a schedule adjustment; late is surgery.

### B3. Hand trucks, dollies, carts, and pallet jacks

The hand truck is the most-used tool in this company and the one people get lazy with.

- **Load low and centered**, heaviest at the bottom. Strap anything stacked over chest height.
- **Push, don't pull**, on the flat. You can see where you're going and your back stays neutral.
- **Down a ramp or incline: keep the load below you and walk backward with it,** controlling the
  descent. Never let a loaded truck get in front of you downhill.
- **Curbs:** approach square, tip back, use the wheels and your legs. Don't yank.
- **Stairs:** use a stair-climber hand truck, take one step at a time, keep the load leaning into
  you. **Never take a loaded hand truck down stairs alone if it's over 100 lb — get a second
  person** on the down-side to control it. Cellar stairs at bar accounts are steep, often wet, and
  usually the worst stairs you'll see all day.
- **Cylinders ride in a cylinder cart or strapped upright** to the hand truck — never balanced
  loose, never rolled on their base across a floor, never carried by the valve or cap.
- **Pallet jacks:** load balanced, forks all the way in, pump only as high as needed. Pull on flats;
  on any slope get the jack *below* you. Watch feet — yours and everyone else's; steel toes don't
  beat 1,500 lb of rolling pallet.
- Park hand trucks and jacks out of walkways, forks down.

### B4. Ladders and working at height

![Ladder set at the 4-to-1 angle, extending 3 feet past the landing, with the climber's belt buckle kept between the rails.](/billing/safety/ladder-angle.svg)

You will be on a ladder in a customer's kitchen, in a cellar, at a dispenser tower, or at a
warehouse rack. Falls from a 4-foot ladder kill people.

**Before you climb:**

- **Inspect it:** rails, rungs, feet, spreaders, locks. Damaged ladder = tagged out, not "careful."
- **Right ladder for the job:** height (you should never need the top two steps), duty rating for
  you plus tools, and **fiberglass only near anything electrical** — no aluminum ladders around
  panels, conduit, or wet electrical.
- **Right surface:** firm, level, dry. Not on a milk crate, pallet, table, or the bed of a truck.
  Not on a floor mat that can slide. If the floor is greasy, clean it first.
- **In a customer space:** cone or block the area. A swinging kitchen door into your ladder is a
  classic and it will put you on the floor.

**On the ladder:**

- **Three points of contact** at all times — two hands + a foot, or two feet + a hand.
- **Face the ladder** going up and down. Never carry tools in your hands while climbing — use a
  tool belt, a bucket and rope, or hand them up.
- **Belt buckle stays between the rails.** Don't lean or reach — move the ladder. "It's just one
  more foot" is the sentence that precedes the fall.
- **Never stand on the top cap or the top step** of a stepladder. Never straddle the top.
- Stepladders get **fully opened with spreaders locked** — never leaned against a wall as a
  straight ladder.
- Extension ladders: 4-to-1 angle (1 foot out for every 4 up), extend 3 feet past the landing, tie
  it off at the top if you can.
- **Never let someone hold the ladder instead of setting it up right.** A person is not a substitute
  for correct footing.

**Anything above roughly 6 feet where you can't use a ladder safely — scaffolding, a lift, or
another approach — stop and call your supervisor.** We do not improvise at height.

### B5. Slips, trips, and falls

Our environments are wet by design. Kitchens have grease. Cellars have condensation. Warehouse
floors get syrup on them.

- **Clean spills immediately** — yours or anyone's, ours or the customer's. If you can't clean it
  now, cone it and tell someone. Walking past a spill is on you if the next person goes down.
- **Syrup is the worst thing on a floor** — it's slick and it doesn't look wet. Any BIB leak gets
  cleaned and rinsed, not just wiped.
- Walk, don't run, and **never carry a load you can't see over**.
- Keep hoses, cords, and straps out of walkways. Coil and hang them.
- Watch for the customer's hazards: floor mats with curled edges, missing drain covers, sunken
  thresholds, dark cellar steps, ice near the ice machine.
- Use handrails on every stair, every time.

### B6. Hand tools, power tools, and knives

- **Right tool for the job.** No pipe wrench as a hammer, no screwdriver as a chisel or pry bar.
- **Inspect before use:** mushroomed heads, split handles, dull or chipped blades — retire them.
- **Guards stay on.** If a guard is in your way, you're doing it wrong or you need a different tool.
- **Unplug or remove the battery to change blades, bits, and accessories.** Every time. This is how
  people lose fingers.
- **Secure the work** — clamp or vise it. One hand holding the piece and one on the tool is how
  you drill through your palm.
- Eye protection for anything that cuts, grinds, drills, or hammers. Hearing protection for
  extended grinding, cutting, or hammer-drilling (§B12).
- Loose clothing, long hair, and jewelry get secured around anything that spins.
- **Cords:** inspect for cuts and exposed conductors; never carry a tool by the cord or yank the
  cord to unplug; keep cords out of water and out of walkways.
- **Compressed air is for tools and parts, never for blowing dust off your clothing or skin,** and
  never pointed at a person. It embolizes.
- **Box cutters and knives:** cut *away* from your body, retract the blade the instant the cut ends,
  and replace dull blades — dull blades cause more injuries than sharp ones because you push harder.
  Never leave an exposed blade on a truck shelf, in a tote, or in your pocket.

### B7. Electrical safety

We work around electricity in wet places — that combination is why this section exists.

- **Only qualified people open equipment past the plug.** If it needs a panel opened, a circuit
  traced, or hardwiring touched, that's an electrician or a qualified tech — not "I'll just look."
- **Assume every circuit is live until you prove otherwise** by test, and lock it out (§F1).
- **Dry hands, dry footing.** If you're standing in water, you don't touch electrical anything —
  fix the water first.
- **GFCI protection** for any tool used outdoors, near a sink, in a cellar, or anywhere damp. If
  the GFCI trips, that's a fault to find, not a nuisance to bypass.
- **Extension cords are temporary.** Rated for the load, not run through doorways or under mats, not
  daisy-chained, not through water. Unplug and coil them when the job's done.
- **Damaged cord or cracked plug = tagged out.** Not taped, not "it works if you hold it."
- **Keep electrical panels clear** — 36 inches of clearance, nothing stored against them, at our
  warehouse and at customer sites. If a customer has stored product against their panel, tell them
  and note it.
- **Never use water on an electrical fire.** CO₂ or dry-chemical extinguisher, and kill power if you
  can do it safely.
- Report any shock, tingle, or buzz — however small — immediately. A "little" shock means a real
  fault that will eventually be a big one.

### B8. Fire prevention and extinguishers

![PASS: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side.](/billing/safety/pass-extinguisher.svg)

- Extinguishers at Hangar 200 are mounted **every 100 feet**; know the two nearest your work area
  and keep them unobstructed. Know where they are at every customer account you work regularly.
- **PASS: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze, Sweep side to side.**
- **Fight a fire only if:** it's smaller than a trash can, you have a clear exit at your back, the
  alarm has been raised, and you have the right extinguisher. Otherwise get out. Equipment is
  insured; you are not replaceable.
- Flammables (solvents, cements, aerosols, brazing gases) stay closed, upright, away from ignition
  sources and out of the battery-charging area.
- **Oily and solvent-soaked rags self-ignite.** They go in the closed metal rag can — never in a
  pile, a bucket, an open trash bin, or the back of your truck.
- Never block or disable sprinklers, alarm pull stations, or exits. Nothing stored within 18 inches
  of a sprinkler head.
- Smoking only in designated outdoor areas, never near the CO₂ pad, the charging area, or any
  flammable storage.

### B9. Hazard communication and chemicals

Every hazardous chemical on site has a Safety Data Sheet in our digital SDS library — searchable in
the compliance app, cached offline on the tablet, with the paper binder in the front office as
backup. **Small quantities count**: a single can of spray paint has an SDS.

- **Read the label before you use anything.** GHS pictograms: flame = flammable; corrosion = burns
  skin and eyes; exclamation = irritant; skull = acutely toxic; starburst torso = long-term health
  hazard; cylinder = gas under pressure.
- **Never use an unlabeled container, and never pour into one.** If you decant it, you label it —
  product name and hazard, in writing.
- **Never mix cleaning chemicals.** Specifically, in our world:
  - **Bleach + any acid = chlorine gas.** Our ice-machine cleaners (Manitowoc, Super-Kool) are
    acids. Keep them and bleach separated in use *and* in storage.
  - **Bleach + ammonia = chloramine gas.** Glass cleaners can contain ammonia.
  - **Chlorinating tablets (trichlor) are a strong oxidizer.** Dry storage, own container, away from
    every liquid chemical — moisture or acid contact releases chlorine gas.
- **Caustic line-cleaning chemicals burn skin and eyes** and you often use them in cramped spaces
  (§F5). Full PPE, no exceptions, and know where the eyewash is before you open the jug.
- Solvent cements, primers, and aerosols are flammable and give off vapor — ventilate, keep away
  from ignition, close containers between uses.
- **New chemical on site or on your truck?** Tell the EH&S Lead the same week so its SDS gets filed
  before it goes on a shelf. If a purchase would put us over 55 gallons (liquid), 500 lb (solid), or
  200 cubic feet (gas) of anything, the EH&S Lead must know **first** — that changes our CERS filing
  and there's a 30-day clock on it.

### B10. Compressed gas — CO₂, nitrogen, and beer gas

![Cylinders stored upright, chained, and capped, with full and empty segregated — versus freestanding or lying loose.](/billing/safety/cylinder-handling.svg)

Compressed gas is our signature hazard. It is invisible, it is under enormous pressure, and it
does not smell like anything.

**Two separate dangers, both real:**

1. **Asphyxiation.** CO₂ and nitrogen displace oxygen. CO₂ is *heavier than air* and pools in low
   spots — cellars, pits, walk-ins, stairwells, the bottom of a closed truck. You will not smell it.
   You may not feel it coming. See §D4 — this is the section that saves lives.
2. **Stored energy.** A cylinder is a bomb if the valve is broken off. A knocked-over cylinder with
   a sheared valve becomes a projectile that goes through cinder block.

**Handling rules, everywhere, always:**

- **Upright and secured** — chained, racked, or in a cylinder cart. Never freestanding, never lying
  loose.
- **Valve cap on** whenever the cylinder isn't connected and in use. The cap is the valve's armor.
- **Never lift or drag a cylinder by the valve or cap.**
- **Never roll a cylinder on its side.** Tilt-and-roll on the base edge is acceptable for short
  moves on flat ground; a cart is better.
- **Full and empty segregated and labeled.** An "empty" still holds pressure.
- **Open valves slowly.** Point the outlet away from you and everyone else. Never stand in front of
  a regulator when pressurizing.
- **Frostbite:** liquid CO₂ and cold gas will freeze skin instantly. Insulated gloves and eye
  protection for any connect, disconnect, or fill.
- **Never use oil or grease on CO₂ or oxygen fittings.**
- **Leak check with soapy water**, never a flame. A hissing cylinder gets moved outdoors if it's
  safe to do so, and the area evacuated if it isn't.
- **In a vehicle:** upright, secured, valve closed, cap on, and **ventilation**. Never in a sealed
  cab. Never leave cylinders in a closed vehicle in the sun.

### B11. Heat illness prevention (8 CCR §3395)

Applies to warehouse work in summer, outdoor CO₂ fills, and every delivery route.

- **Water:** drink often — roughly a cup every 15–20 minutes in heat. Don't wait for thirst. Water
  is provided; if it runs out, that's an immediate report.
- **Rest and shade:** take a cool-down break in shade or the air-conditioned office whenever you
  need one. **No permission required, no explanation required.** A supervisor who discourages it is
  violating this handbook.
- **Acclimatize:** your first week in heat, and during any heat wave, your body isn't adapted. Pace
  yourself, say so, and supervisors will adjust the schedule.
- **Know the signs:**
  - **Heat exhaustion** — heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps, weakness. Stop, get
    to shade or AC, drink water, tell someone, don't drive.
  - **Heat stroke** — confusion, slurred speech, no sweating, hot red skin, collapse. **Call 911
    immediately.** Cool them aggressively with water and ice, and do not leave them alone. This
    kills people fast.
- Never leave anyone alone in a hot enclosed space — truck cab, container, or unventilated cellar.
- Watch each other. The person with heat stroke is usually the last to know.

### B12. Noise

Extended grinding, cutting, hammer-drilling, and shop work can exceed safe levels. Rule of thumb:
if you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone at arm's length, wear hearing protection.
Plugs and muffs are stocked in the tool room. Hearing loss is cumulative and permanent.

### B13. Housekeeping and sanitation

Housekeeping is safety, not tidiness.

- Aisles and exits clear. Nothing stored blocking an extinguisher, eyewash, electrical panel,
  alarm station, or the CO₂ shutoff (#4461).
- Nothing within 18 inches of a sprinkler head.
- Pallets stored flat, not on edge. Broken pallets out of service.
- Clean as you go. Cardboard, banding, and shrink wrap get picked up immediately — banding on a
  floor is a trip hazard and a tangle hazard for the forklift.
- Restrooms, handwashing, and drinking water are provided and maintained. Report anything out of
  service.

### B14. Workplace violence and security (SB 553 / Labor Code §6401.9)

California requires a Workplace Violence Prevention Plan. This is your part of it.

- **Report threats, intimidation, harassment, or violence** — from anyone: coworker, customer,
  delivery driver, patron, stranger — to your supervisor or the EH&S Lead immediately. Reports are
  retaliation-protected. "I wasn't sure it was serious enough" is exactly what we want reported.
- **An angry or erratic person on site:** don't engage, don't block their exit, don't touch them.
  Create distance, get to another area or your vehicle, alert a supervisor. **911 for anything
  physical or any weapon.**
- **In bar and nightclub accounts** you will encounter intoxicated people. Do not argue, do not
  match energy, do not intervene in someone else's conflict. Find the manager. If the manager can't
  control the situation, leave and call your supervisor — we will reschedule the account.
- Doors and gates at Hangar 200 stay controlled. Don't badge in people you don't know. Don't leave
  the warehouse open and unattended.
- Every incident gets logged and investigated, and the plan gets adjusted — same philosophy as
  near-misses (§G5).

### B15. Fitness for duty

- **No alcohol or drugs on the job, on our property, or in our vehicles.** This includes cannabis,
  legal or not, and includes being under the influence from off-hours use.
- **Prescription and over-the-counter medication** that causes drowsiness or impairs judgment: tell
  your supervisor *before* your shift. We will find you work you can do safely. That conversation is
  always safe to have and stays private.
- **Fatigue impairs like alcohol.** If you're too tired to drive safely, say so. We will get the
  route covered. Driving a truck fatigued to finish a route is never worth it.
- Post-incident testing may apply after a vehicle collision or serious injury per company policy.

## Part C — Driving and the road

Most of our people spend more time in a vehicle than anywhere else. Vehicle incidents are the
highest-severity risk in this company.

### C1. Before you drive

- **Valid license** in your possession, appropriate for the vehicle. Tell us immediately if your
  license is suspended, restricted, or your medical status changes — driving without one puts you
  and the company in a very bad place.
- **Pre-trip walk-around, every day, before the first move:**
  - Tires (pressure, tread, damage) and wheels
  - Lights: headlights, brake, turn, hazards, backup, clearance
  - Mirrors clean and adjusted
  - Fluids and any leak under the vehicle
  - Brakes and parking brake
  - Windshield, wipers, horn
  - **Cargo area:** load secured, straps and load bars in place, nothing loose that becomes a
    projectile in a panic stop
  - **Cylinders: upright, secured, valves closed, caps on, ventilation** (§B10)
  - Fire extinguisher, cones, first-aid kit, spill kit present
- **Load securement is not optional.** A 50 lb cylinder loose in a box truck during a 30 mph stop
  hits like a motorcycle. Strap it, block it, or don't move.
- **Distribute weight evenly**, heavy low and forward of the axle. Don't overload the liftgate side.

### C2. Driving rules

- **Seatbelt, always, everyone, every trip** — including the 200 feet between two loading docks.
- **No handheld phone. Ever.** Not at a light, not "just a text," not for the route app. Pull over
  or use a hands-free mount set before you move. This is a terminable offense on a second occurrence.
- **Following distance:** 4+ seconds in a loaded box truck on dry pavement; double it in rain, at
  night, or with a heavy load. Trucks don't stop like cars and ours are usually loaded.
- **Speed:** at or under the limit, and slower than the limit when conditions say so. We do not run
  routes on a stopwatch. If the schedule is making you drive fast, that's a scheduling problem to
  report, not a driving problem to solve.
- **Intersections:** cover the brake, look left-right-left, and expect the red-light runner.
- **Bicycles and pedestrians** — assume they're in your blind spot in Oakland, Berkeley, SF, and
  Alameda, because they usually are. Check mirrors twice before any right turn.
- **Wide turns:** swing wide late, not early; watch your trailer end and your mirrors sweeping poles
  and parked cars.
- No eating, no route paperwork, no radio fiddling while moving.

### C3. Backing

Backing causes a large share of all commercial vehicle incidents, and nearly all of them are
avoidable.

- **Avoid backing when you can.** Pull through. Choose a spot you can drive out of.
- **GOAL — Get Out And Look.** Every time, before every back. Walk the full path. Check overhead,
  behind, both sides, and the ground for people, bollards, low walls, and children.
- **Use a spotter** whenever one is available. Agree on hand signals first. **If you lose sight of
  your spotter, stop immediately.**
- Back slowly, idle speed, mirrors both sides, window down so you can hear.
- Tap the horn twice before starting to back.
- When in doubt, get out and look again. Nobody has ever been fired for taking an extra 60 seconds.

### C4. Height, weight, and clearance

- **Know your vehicle's height and post it on the dash.** A box truck into a parking garage,
  drive-thru, or low bridge is a career-defining mistake and it happens to people who "knew" they'd
  fit.
- **Never enter a parking structure in a box truck** unless clearance is posted, verified, and you
  have room to turn around. Park on the street and use the hand truck.
- Watch for low overhangs at loading docks, canopies at restaurants, tree limbs on residential
  streets, and awnings at bar entrances.
- Respect posted weight limits on ramps, docks, and older loading areas.

### C5. Parking, double parking, cones, and the bike lane

![Overhead view of a temporary lane stop: hazard flashers, cones set front and rear before unloading, work from the curb side, bike lane kept clear.](/billing/safety/double-park-cones.svg)

City delivery is where our drivers face the most exposure. These rules exist because the street is
the most dangerous workplace we have.

**Choosing a spot — in order of preference:**

1. A legal commercial loading zone (yellow), even if it means a longer walk.
2. A legal parking space.
3. A safe temporary stop with full precautions (below) — last resort, shortest possible time.

**Never park:**

- **In a bike lane.** Ever. This is the single most dangerous thing a delivery driver does in the
  East Bay — it forces cyclists into a traffic lane at exactly the point where they're beside your
  blind spot. Find another spot, even if it costs you ten minutes.
- Blocking a crosswalk, ADA ramp, fire hydrant, or fire lane.
- Blocking a driveway or an intersection sight line.
- On the opposite side of the street from the account, requiring you to cross traffic with a loaded
  hand truck.

**If you must stop temporarily in a travel lane ("double park"):**

- **Hazard flashers on before you stop.**
- **Cones out** — minimum two, one at the rear corner toward traffic and one forward, set *before*
  you begin unloading. Cones are the difference between a driver seeing "truck" and seeing "work
  happening, move over."
- **Park as far right as possible**, wheels straight, engine off, keys with you, parking brake set.
- **Work from the curb side.** Never open the street-side door into traffic, and never stage product
  on the traffic side of the truck.
- **Keep the stop short.** Get in, get done, get out. If the delivery is a long one, move the truck
  to a legal spot and walk it.
- **Retrieve your cones every single time.** A cone left behind is a hazard and a ticket.
- Understand that a citation is possible and is not your fault when you followed this procedure —
  **we pay legitimate loading tickets; we do not pay for unsafe parking.** Never take a risk with
  your body to avoid a ticket.

**Crossing traffic with a load:** don't. If you have to cross a street with a hand truck, use the
crosswalk, make eye contact with drivers, and take the load in two trips rather than one blind one.

### C6. Loading and unloading curbside

- **Set up before you open the door:** flashers, cones, hi-vis vest on.
- Stand on the **curb side** of the load wherever possible. Never with your back to traffic.
- Lower the ramp or gate fully and confirm it's stable before rolling anything onto it.
- **Watch the ground:** curb cuts, broken sidewalk, storm grates that eat hand-truck wheels,
  cellar doors in the sidewalk (sidewalk hatches are common at bar accounts and they are not always
  latched — never stand on one).
- Don't leave the truck open and unattended with product visible. Close and lock between trips.
- Never leave a load unattended on a public sidewalk.

### C7. Liftgates

- Inspect before use: hydraulic leaks, bent platform, worn hinge pins, controls.
- **Never exceed the rated capacity** — it's on the plate.
- Load **centered** on the platform, wheels chocked or strapped so nothing rolls.
- **Never ride the gate.** Never let a customer ride it either.
- Keep hands and feet clear of the pinch points at the hinges and the platform edge.
- Full stop, platform fully down and flat, before rolling anything off.
- Stow and latch the gate before moving the vehicle. A gate that drops on the freeway is a fatality.

### C8. Collisions — exactly what to do

Keep this sequence in the truck. You will not think clearly right after an impact.

**1 — Stop and make it safe.**
- Stop immediately. Leaving the scene of any collision is a crime, however minor.
- Hazards on. Move to a safe location **only** if the vehicle is drivable and staying is dangerous.
- **Cones out** behind the vehicle to protect the scene.
- Turn off the engine. Set the brake.

**2 — People first.**
- Check yourself, then your passenger, then the other vehicle, then bystanders.
- **Call 911 for any injury, any airbag deployment, any fluid leak, any blocked lane.** When in
  doubt, call. "I felt fine at the scene" is how people discover injuries the next day with no
  record.
- Don't move an injured person unless they're in immediate danger (fire, traffic).

**3 — Call the office. Same call, every time.** Your supervisor, then Sky (415) 713-3318 if you
can't reach them. We will walk you through the rest and get you help. Do this before you negotiate
anything with anyone.

**4 — What NOT to say.**
- **Never admit fault. Never say "I'm sorry," "my fault," or "I didn't see you."** Those are legal
  admissions, and at the scene you genuinely don't know all the facts yet.
- Don't argue, don't accept blame, don't promise to pay for anything, don't agree to "handle it
  without insurance."
- Be polite and factual with police. Answer what's asked. If you don't know, say you don't know.

**5 — Collect.**
- Other driver: name, phone, address, license number, insurance carrier and policy number, plate,
  vehicle make/model/color.
- **Photos:** all vehicles from multiple angles, all damage close and wide, plates, the whole scene
  including signs, signals, lane markings, skid marks, and road conditions. More photos than you
  think you need.
- **Witnesses:** name and phone. Do it immediately — witnesses leave fast.
- Police officer's name, badge, and report number.

**6 — Afterward.**
- Report to the office the same day, in writing, even for a scratch in a parking lot.
- Post-incident testing may apply per company policy.
- Expect an incident review — it's about what we change, not about punishing you.

**Any contact with another vehicle, person, structure, or fixed object counts** — including
backing into a bollard, clipping a mirror, or bumping a dock. Report it. Undisclosed damage found
later is a far bigger problem than the damage.

### C9. Breakdowns and roadside emergencies

- Get as far off the roadway as you can. Hazards on immediately.
- **Cones or triangles out** behind the vehicle — further back on a highway (give drivers time to
  react at speed).
- **Stay in the vehicle with your seatbelt on** if you're in a live traffic lane or on a freeway
  shoulder — outside the vehicle on a freeway shoulder is one of the most dangerous places you can
  stand. Exit on the **non-traffic side** if you must get out.
- Call the office. Don't attempt roadside repairs on a freeway shoulder.
- Never change a tire on the traffic side of a vehicle on a busy road.

### C10. Vehicle security and personal safety on route

- **Lock the cab every time you leave it**, even for a two-minute drop. Keys with you, never in the
  ignition, never "hidden" on a tire.
- Don't leave tools, phones, laptops, or paperwork visible. Tools go in locked boxes.
- **Park in lit, visible areas** for early-morning and evening stops. Back into spots so you can
  pull straight out.
- **Situational awareness on approach:** look at the block before you commit. If something feels
  wrong, it is — drive on, call your supervisor, we reschedule. **Your judgment about a location is
  final and will never be second-guessed.**
- **If you are robbed: give them what they want.** Product, cash, keys, the truck. None of it is
  worth injury. Don't chase, don't argue, don't be a hero. Get to safety, call 911, then call us.
- Catalytic converter and fuel theft happen in our area — report anything you notice, and don't
  confront anyone.
- Late-night and early-morning bar deliveries: know your route in, don't prop doors open behind
  you, and keep your phone on you rather than in the cab.

## Part D — Working in customer accounts

We are guests in someone else's workplace, and their hazards become ours the moment we walk in.

### D1. Arriving at an account

- **Check in with the manager on duty.** Always. They need to know you're there, where you'll be
  working, and if you'll block anything.
- **Take 30 seconds to look around.** Where are the exits? Where's the electrical panel? Is the
  floor wet? Is there a cellar? Is the kitchen mid-rush? That look-around is a required part of the
  job, not an optional courtesy.
- **Cone or block your work area** before you start — especially anything in a walkway, a doorway,
  or in front of a swinging door.
- Wear your hi-vis and keep your work area tight. Tools and parts stay in your bag or on a mat, not
  scattered across their floor.
- If the account is mid-rush and the work can wait 20 minutes, wait. A crowded kitchen during service
  is a genuinely more dangerous place, for you and for them.

### D2. Bars and nightclubs

- **Expect broken glass** — on the floor, in ice bins, in the well. Never reach blindly into an ice
  bin, a well, or under a bar counter. Look first, and use a light.
- **Floors behind the bar are wet, slick, and covered with rubber mats** with curled edges. Move
  deliberately.
- **Lighting is bad.** Carry a headlamp or flashlight. Don't work in the dark because it's
  "quicker."
- **Space is cramped** — bars are built for bartenders, not for a tech with a hand truck. Plan your
  path, move barstools out of the way, and ask staff to clear a lane.
- **Intoxicated patrons:** do not engage. Don't accept drinks. Don't get pulled into conflicts. If a
  patron interferes with your work, get the manager. See §B14.
- **Late-night and early-morning:** door propped, alley entrances, and empty streets — see §C10.
- **CO₂ in bars lives in the cellar or a back room.** Read §D4 before you go down there. This is not
  optional reading.

### D3. Restaurants and commercial kitchens

- **Hot surfaces everywhere:** fryers, grills, steam tables, dish machines, hot water lines. Assume
  every metal surface is hot until you know otherwise. Never set a tool on a range top.
- **Fryers are the worst hazard in the room.** Never work over, beside, or reaching across a hot
  fryer. If your job requires being near one, ask that it be shut off and given time to cool — and
  wait for it.
- **Grease on floors** is more slippery than water and doesn't look wet. Non-slip footwear is
  mandatory and still not enough — walk deliberately.
- **Knives** are everywhere, often on prep surfaces or in sinks. Never reach into a sink of cloudy
  water.
- **Staff move fast, carrying hot and sharp things, and cannot always see you.** Announce yourself
  — "behind you," "coming through." Never stand in a doorway or a blind corner.
- Watch for **overhead hazards**: heat lamps, low shelving, hanging pots, open cabinet doors.
- Gas lines and pilot lights: if you smell gas, everyone leaves and the gas company gets called.
  Don't hunt for it, don't flip switches.
- **Never move or unplug a customer's equipment** without telling the manager — you can dump a
  walk-in of product or trip a circuit that runs their POS.

### D4. Basements, cellars, and CO₂ accumulation — read this twice

![Cross-section of a bar cellar: CO₂ sinks and fills the space from the floor up, displacing oxygen. Stop at the top of the stairs; if someone is down, do not go in.](/billing/safety/co2-cellar.svg)

**This is the section most likely to save your life.**

CO₂ is heavier than air. When it leaks — from a cylinder, a regulator, a line, or a bulk tank — it
**sinks and fills the lowest space from the floor up**. Bar cellars, restaurant basements, walk-in
floors, stairwells, and pits are exactly where it collects. You cannot see it or smell it. Oxygen
displacement can drop you unconscious in seconds with no warning, and people who go down the stairs
to help the first person become the second and third victims.

**Every time, before entering any cellar, basement, pit, or below-grade room where gas is stored:**

1. **Stop at the top of the stairs.** Look and listen — hissing, frost on a fitting, an alarm
   sounding, a CO₂ monitor showing a fault.
2. **Ventilate first.** Open the doors and hatches. If the space has a fan, run it. Give it real
   time — minutes, not seconds.
3. **Never enter alone.** Someone knows you're going down and stays where they can see or hear you.
4. **If the space has a CO₂ monitor, confirm it reads normal.** Many accounts now have them. A
   monitor in alarm means *do not enter*, full stop, no matter what the manager says.
5. **If anything feels wrong — headache, dizziness, shortness of breath, rapid breathing, a feeling
   of "I need to get out" — GET OUT IMMEDIATELY** and up to fresh air. Those are the symptoms of
   oxygen displacement and they get worse fast. Never push through them.

**If you find someone down in a cellar or enclosed space:**

- **DO NOT GO IN.** This is the single hardest rule in this handbook and the most important. The gas
  that dropped them will drop you in the same number of seconds, and then there are two casualties
  and nobody to call for help.
- **Call 911 immediately** and say the words **"possible CO₂ leak, person down, confined space."**
  That phrasing gets responders with air supplies.
- Open every door and hatch you can reach **from outside** the space.
- Keep everyone else out — physically block the stairs if you have to.
- Call your supervisor.

**We do not do permit-required confined space entry.** If a job seems to require entering a tank, a
vault, or any space that isn't a normally-occupied room, stop and call your supervisor (§F8).

### D5. Walk-in coolers and freezers

- **Know how the door opens from the inside before you let it close behind you.** Every walk-in has
  an inside release; find it first. If it's broken or blocked, don't go in — tell the manager.
- **Never work in a walk-in alone without someone knowing** you're in there and roughly how long.
- **Prop the door** with something that won't be moved by a passing employee, and tell staff you're
  in there.
- Freezer work: cold injury is real. Take breaks out of the cold, wear gloves, and don't touch bare
  metal or evaporator coils with wet hands.
- Floors are slick with ice and condensation. Watch your footing constantly.
- **Refrigerant leak in an enclosed cooler** displaces oxygen just like CO₂ — same rules as §D4:
  get out, ventilate, don't re-enter.

### D6. Customer equipment, customer ladders, customer help

- **Don't use the customer's ladder.** You don't know its history or condition, and if it fails
  it's still your body. Use ours.
- **Don't use the customer's tools.** Same reason.
- **Don't let customer staff help you lift, carry, or hold anything.** They're not trained, they're
  not covered by our insurance, and if they get hurt helping us it's a serious problem for
  everyone. Politely decline: *"Thanks — I've got it, company policy."*
- **Don't service equipment that isn't ours** without authorization. If their fryer, their walk-in,
  or their electrical looks unsafe, tell the manager and note it — don't fix it.
- **Don't move their product, block their prep line, or use their prep surfaces** as a workbench.

### D7. Food safety in customer spaces

We work in food establishments and can contaminate food as easily as any employee there.

- **Wash your hands** before starting work on any dispensing equipment and after handling anything
  dirty. Use their handwash sink, not the prep sink or the mop sink.
- **No open beverages, food, gum, or tobacco** in prep areas.
- Keep tools, bags, and parts off food-contact and prep surfaces — use a mat or a cart.
- **Nothing that touches the floor touches the dispenser.** If a part hits the floor, it gets
  cleaned and sanitized before it goes back in.
- Sanitize hands and connectors before making syrup or water connections. Follow the sanitizer
  contact times in the product guides — a rinse is not a sanitize.
- **Never use compressed air or a chemical near open food or an open ice bin.** Cover or have the
  ice removed.
- If you contaminate anything — dropped part in the ice, chemical near open product — **tell the
  manager immediately.** They may need to dump the ice or the batch. Saying nothing is how people
  get sick.
- If you're sick with vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice, sore throat with fever, or an infected cut on
  your hands, **you do not go into a food establishment.** Call in. This is a health-code
  requirement, not a judgment call, and you will not be penalized.

### D8. Damage, spills, and customer property

- **Spill in a customer space:** contain it, clean it, and tell the manager. Syrup on a bar floor is
  a slip hazard for their staff for the rest of the shift.
- **Damage you cause — however minor — gets reported to the manager AND to your supervisor the same
  day, in writing, with photos.** A scuffed door frame reported immediately is a non-event. The same
  scuff discovered a week later is a dispute.
- **Damage you find** (existing dents, a leaking fitting, damaged equipment) gets photographed and
  reported too, so it doesn't get attributed to us later.
- Leave the space cleaner than you found it. Take your packaging, your old parts, and your trash
  with you — including empty cartons and used rags.

### D9. Working alone and after hours

Many of our stops are solo, early, or late.

- Your route or dispatch record is your check-in system — keep it current so we know where you are.
- Tell someone before entering a cellar, walk-in, or any isolated space (§D4, §D5).
- Keep your phone charged and **on your person**, not in the truck.
- If you're going somewhere with no cell service, tell your supervisor first and check in after.
- If an account is closed, dark, or something feels off — leave and call. We reschedule. Always.

## Part E — Warehouse and fill station (Hangar 200)

### E1. Forklift and powered industrial trucks

![Load centre and stability: capacity drops as the load moves out on the forks. If the truck tips, stay in the seat and lean away from the fall.](/billing/safety/forklift-load.svg)

Governed by Cal/OSHA 8 CCR §3650–3669 and federal 29 CFR 1910.178.

**⚠ Reading this handbook does NOT authorize you to operate the forklift.** Operators must complete
formal training — classroom plus hands-on plus a practical evaluation on *our* equipment — be
certified by the employer, and be re-evaluated **at least every 3 years**, after any incident or
near-miss, and when assigned a different truck type. **No certification on file = no keys.** Period.

**Rules for everyone, operator or not:**

- **The forklift has the right of way** — not out of courtesy, because it can't stop and can't see
  you. Make eye contact with the operator before crossing its path.
- **Never walk under raised forks**, loaded or empty.
- **No riders, ever.** No passengers on the truck. No people lifted on forks, pallets, or a jury-rigged
  platform.
- Stay out of the operator's blind side. Assume they haven't seen you until they signal that they have.

**Rules for certified operators:**

- **Pre-operation inspection every shift**, documented: forks, mast, chains, hydraulics (leaks),
  tires, horn, lights, brakes, seatbelt, capacity plate legible, LP/battery secure. **A truck that
  fails inspection is tagged out** — key pulled, supervisor told. Operating a known-defective truck
  is a terminable offense because it is how people die.
- **Seatbelt on before the truck moves.** Tip-over protocol: **stay in the seat, brace, lean away
  from the direction of fall.** Never jump — the overhead guard is what saves you and jumping puts
  you under it.
- **Know your load:** within the capacity plate rating (and remember capacity drops as the load
  center moves out), centered, mast tilted back, forks 4–6 inches off the floor when traveling.
- **Load blocking your view → travel in reverse**, looking in the direction of travel.
- Slow at corners, doorways, and blind intersections; sound the horn. Three truck-lengths behind
  any other moving equipment.
- Never raise or lower a load while moving. Never turn on a grade with a raised load.
- **When parking:** forks flat on the floor, controls neutral, brake set, key out. Chock on any grade.
- **Charging/fueling in the designated area only** — ventilated, no smoking, no open flame, eyewash
  location known before you touch a battery. LP cylinder changes: valve closed, no ignition sources,
  gloves and eye protection.
- **Cylinders, kegs, and equipment move on proper attachments or secured pallets** — never loose on
  bare forks.
- Pedestrian awareness is on the operator too. Assume people will walk out from behind racking.

### E2. Racking and storage

- **Load racks bottom-heavy.** Heaviest product on the lowest beams.
- Never exceed a rack's rated capacity, and never modify racking without approval.
- **Damaged racking gets reported and tagged out immediately** — bent uprights, missing safety clips,
  damaged beams. Racking collapse is catastrophic. "It's been like that a while" is not a reason to
  leave it.
- **Never climb racking.** Use the ladder or the forklift with an approved platform, never the forks
  alone.
- Keep pallets fully on the beams — no overhang that a passing truck can catch.
- Nothing stored within 18 inches of a sprinkler head.

### E3. CO₂ cylinder filling

Filling is our highest-hazard routine operation. **Only trained, designated fill operators fill
cylinders.**

- **Fill outside, at area #2300, per the posted fill procedure.** Never fill in an enclosed space.
- **PPE: safety glasses (or face shield), insulated gloves, closed-toe footwear, no loose clothing.**
- **Inspect every cylinder before filling:** current hydrostatic test date, no dents, gouges, deep
  corrosion, fire damage, or valve damage. **A cylinder that fails inspection does not get filled** —
  it gets tagged and set aside for the supplier. Filling a damaged cylinder is how a cylinder
  becomes a bomb.
- **Never overfill.** Follow the posted weights. Overfilled cylinders vent violently and can rupture.
- Secure the cylinder during filling. Open valves slowly, stand clear of the fill line and the
  regulator face.
- **Watch for frost** on lines and fittings — that's normal on transfer, but a *growing* frost patch
  or hissing means a leak. Shut down, back off, and get the supervisor.
- **The tank's monitoring alarm and its cellular backup are life-safety devices.** If either shows a
  fault, filling stops until it's resolved. If the alarm sounds: **do not investigate — evacuate
  upwind and call the Emergency Coordinator.** Re-entry only after ventilation and an all-clear.
- Airgas manages the 6-ton bulk tank; **Airgas 24-hr emergency (866) 734-3438**. Never attempt a
  repair on the bulk tank yourself.
- Log every fill per the procedure.

### E4. Cylinder handling and storage at the warehouse

All of §B10 applies, plus:

- Storage areas: upright, chained or racked, caps on, **full and empty clearly segregated**.
- Store away from heat, ignition sources, and electrical panels; outdoors and shaded where possible.
- Aisles kept clear so a leaking cylinder can be reached and removed.
- **The one or two cylinders kept indoors stay within the CO₂ monitor's coverage area.** That's not
  a preference — indoor CO₂ storage is exactly what an inspector checks and exactly where an
  accumulation would hurt someone.
- Never accept a delivery of, or put into service, a cylinder that's out of hydro date or visibly
  damaged.

### E5. Equipment dismantling, scrap, and reman

- **Before cutting into ANY sealed cooling system: check the nameplate refrigerant** (§F2). CO₂/R744
  is exempt from the venting ban; **R134a, R290, R600a and the rest are not** — recover first, log
  it, and drain the compressor oil into the labeled Used Oil container (§G4).
- **R290 (propane) and R600a (isobutane) are flammable refrigerants.** No hot work, no grinding, no
  ignition source near a system that contains them until it's fully recovered and purged.
- De-energize and verify before disassembly (§F1). Discharge capacitors.
- **Sharp sheet metal is the main injury here** — cut-resistant gloves, long sleeves, and control
  every edge you create. Deburr or fold edges before stacking.
- Glass doors and panels: two-person handling, and don't lay glass flat where someone can step on it.
- Insulation foam and fiberglass: gloves, long sleeves, and a dust mask if it's friable.
- Keep the scrap area organized — a scrap pile is a trip, cut, and crush hazard all at once.

### E6. Receiving and shipping

- Chock trailer wheels and confirm the trailer is secured before entering with a forklift or jack.
- Inspect pallets before moving — broken stringers drop loads.
- Check for damaged or leaking product on receipt. Leaking chemical containers get isolated, not
  restacked.
- Verify SDS exists for any chemical product arriving that we haven't stocked before (§B9).

## Part F — Service and installation trades

### F1. Lockout / de-energize before service (8 CCR §3314)

Before servicing anything with moving parts, stored energy, or a power source:

1. **Notify** anyone affected — including customer staff whose equipment is going down.
2. **Shut down** the equipment normally.
3. **Isolate** the energy: unplug it, or open the breaker/disconnect.
4. **Lock and tag it** where a breaker is involved. Cord-and-plug equipment: **the plug stays under
   your personal control** — in your sight or in your pocket, not just "unplugged" where a helpful
   employee can plug it back in.
5. **Verify dead** — try the start button, test the circuit.
6. **Release stored energy:** bleed pressure, discharge capacitors, block anything that can fall or
   spring.

**Never remove someone else's lock or tag.** Never rely on someone's word that a circuit is off.
Never bypass an interlock "just to see it run."

### F2. Refrigeration and refrigerant (Clean Air Act §608)

- **Venting refrigerant is a federal crime.** Recovery only, by an **EPA 608-certified technician**,
  with proper recovery equipment. Company certification on file: **PA620014460**.
- Know the refrigerant before you open the system — nameplate first, every time.
- **Recovery cylinders: never fill past 80%.** Use a scale. An overfilled recovery cylinder is a
  rupture hazard as ambient temperature rises.
- Never mix refrigerants in a recovery cylinder. Label cylinders correctly.
- **Refrigerant contact freezes skin and eyes instantly** — gloves and eye protection for every
  connection and disconnection.
- **Refrigerant in an enclosed space displaces oxygen** (§D4/§D5 rules apply) and can decompose into
  toxic gases near a flame or hot surface. Ventilate before and during.
- **Flammable refrigerants (R290/R600a)**: no ignition source, no hot work, ventilate, and follow the
  equipment manufacturer's specific procedure.
- Log leak repairs and recovery per our records requirements.

### F3. Brazing, soldering, and hot work

![Hot work zone: 35 feet of clearance, extinguisher within arm's reach, and a 30-minute fire watch after the last flame.](/billing/safety/hot-work-zone.svg)

Hot work causes fires hours after the torch is off. That's why the fire watch exists.

**Before you light a torch:**

- **Get authorization.** Hot work in a customer's building requires the manager's knowledge, and may
  require their own hot-work permit — ask.
- **Clear 35 feet** of combustibles, or cover them with fire blankets. Look *behind* and *below* the
  wall — heat conducts and a stud cavity or an adjacent shelf can smolder.
- **Fire extinguisher within arm's reach** — not down the hall.
- Know where the nearest alarm pull and exit are.
- **Never braze on a system that hasn't been recovered and purged** — pressurized refrigerant plus
  flame produces toxic decomposition gases and can rupture the line.
- **Nitrogen purge while brazing** where the procedure calls for it — and use a **regulator with a
  relief**; never connect a high-pressure nitrogen cylinder directly to a system. Trapped nitrogen
  pressure has killed technicians.

**During:**

- Shaded eye protection, leather gloves, long sleeves, cotton or FR — **no synthetics** (they melt
  into skin).
- Ventilate. Brazing fumes, especially anything with cadmium or from coated metal, are toxic —
  never braze in a closed cellar without airflow.
- Oxy-fuel: cylinders upright and secured, caps on when moved, **no oil or grease on oxygen
  fittings**, hoses inspected, flashback arrestors in place, valves closed and lines bled when done.

**After:**

- **Fire watch for at least 30 minutes** after the last flame — longer if the area is enclosed or
  combustible. Someone stays and watches, with the extinguisher.
- Check the far side of walls and floors you were working near.
- Confirm cylinders are closed and secured before you leave.

### F4. Pressurized systems, leak testing, and vacuum

- **Never pressure-test with oxygen or acetylene.** Ever. Nitrogen or dry air only.
- **Always use a pressure regulator with a relief valve**, set below the weakest component's rating.
- Never exceed the equipment's test pressure. Stand clear while pressurizing; bring pressure up
  slowly.
- **Leak-check with soap solution or an electronic detector — never a flame.**
- **Fully depressurize and verify at zero before opening any fitting.** A pressurized cap
  becomes a projectile.
- Vacuum pumps: keep oil clean and current; **used vacuum pump oil from refrigerant service is
  California hazardous waste** and goes in the labeled Used Oil container (§G4), never in the trash
  or a drain.
- Never stand over a sight glass or gauge while pressurizing.

### F5. Line cleaning and chemical service work

Line cleaning uses caustics and acids in cramped, wet, poorly lit spaces — a bad combination.

- **Full PPE: safety glasses AND face shield, chemical gloves, apron.** Splash-back off a coupler at
  face height is the classic injury.
- **Know where the nearest eyewash and running water are before you open the container.** In a bar
  cellar that may be the mop sink upstairs — find it first.
- Read the SDS for the product you're using — concentrations and contact times vary (§B9).
- **Never mix products.** Never top off one chemical's container with another.
- Ventilate. Never mix chemicals in a closed cellar.
- Flush lines thoroughly and verify no chemical remains before returning the system to service —
  **a customer serving a caustic-contaminated drink is a catastrophic outcome.** When in doubt,
  flush again and taste-test per the procedure.
- Transport chemicals upright, secured, in original labeled containers, separated from food product
  in the vehicle.
- Dispose of rinse water per the procedure — **never down a storm drain** (§G4).

### F6. Ice machines and sanitizers

- **De-energize before servicing** — ice machines have augers, and the harvest cycle can start while
  your hand is inside (§F1).
- Ice machine cleaners are **acids**; sanitizers are often quat or chlorine — **keep them apart**
  (§B9) and never mix them in a bucket or a machine.
- **Sharp evaporator fins and edges** — gloves.
- Never chip ice with a screwdriver or a knife. Use the proper tool.
- After cleaning, run and discard the required cycles before the machine goes back into service.

### F7. Installations — drilling, anchoring, running lines

- **Know what's behind the wall before you drill.** Electrical, gas, water, and refrigerant lines all
  live in walls. Use a scanner, ask the manager, and drill shallow first.
- **Anchoring adhesives (Sika and similar)** contain silica and sensitizers — gloves, eye protection,
  ventilation, and read both parts' SDS (§B9).
- Overhead drilling: face shield plus glasses; nothing above you that can fall on someone below.
- **Cordless over corded** in wet areas; GFCI if corded (§B7).
- Secure the ladder or platform properly (§B4) — installs are where people improvise at height
  because "it's just one hole."
- Route lines so they can't be walked on, rolled over, or snagged. Protect penetrations and seal
  them.
- Clean up drill dust and debris — especially in a food environment (§D7).

### F8. Confined and restricted spaces

**We do not perform permit-required confined space entry.** If a job appears to require entering a
tank, a vault, a pit, a crawlspace, or any space not designed for people to occupy — **stop and call
your supervisor.** We will bring in a qualified contractor or find another approach.

Restricted-but-normally-occupied spaces (cellars, small equipment rooms, walk-ins) are governed by
§D4 and §D5: ventilate first, never alone, get out at the first symptom.

## Part G — When something goes wrong

### G1. Injuries — first aid, treatment, and the claim

**Every injury gets reported the same day** — including "it's nothing" cuts, tweaks, and strains.
Late reporting hurts *your* workers'-comp claim and hides a hazard from the next person.

**Where to go:**

| Situation | Where |
|---|---|
| Life- or limb-threatening; head injury; anyone unconscious; suspected CO₂/refrigerant exposure | **Call 9-1-1.** Nearest ER: Alameda Hospital, 2070 Clinton Ave — (510) 522-3700 |
| Any other work-related injury or illness | **Concentra Oakland** — downtown 384 Embarcadero W, (510) 465-9565 (Mon–Fri 8–5); airport 333 Hegenberger Rd Ste 100, (510) 638-0701 |
| Minor — first aid only | On-site kit (inner hallway), then tell your supervisor so it's logged |

**The sequence:**

1. **Get safe and get first aid.** Chemical in the eye = **15 minutes** at the eyewash (main
   bathroom) before anything else. Bleeding = pressure. Burn = cool water.
2. **Tell your supervisor immediately** — before you drive anywhere. They arrange transport; do not
   drive yourself if you're impaired, in pain, or on medication.
3. **We give you a DWC-1 workers'-comp claim form within one working day** of learning about the
   injury. Fill out the employee section and return it. Your treatment is covered.
4. **Serious injury, amputation, loss of an eye, or any hospitalization: the EH&S Lead is notified
   immediately** — Cal/OSHA requires our report **immediately, and no later than 8 hours**, on the
   statewide line **1-833-579-0927**. (The (916) 263-2800 number on our ERCP is a general
   Sacramento line, not the reporting line.)
5. **The scene stays as-is** where practical until it's photographed, unless leaving it is unsafe.

You will not be disciplined for getting hurt. You may be disciplined for hiding it.

### G3. Emergencies at a customer site

- **Call 911 first** — you're in someone else's building and they may not know what's happening.
- **Tell the manager on duty** so they can evacuate their staff and customers.
- **Then call your supervisor.**
- For a CO₂ or refrigerant release: get everyone out and *up*, keep people away from the cellar or
  low space, and tell responders what gas and roughly how much (§D4).
- Don't attempt to fight a fire or stop a large leak beyond what §B8 allows.

### G4. Spills and releases

- **Small, and you're trained and equipped:** contain with absorbent, ventilate, tell your
  supervisor. Used absorbent goes into labeled hazardous-waste storage — **not the dumpster**.
- **Large, unknown, a gas, or you're not sure:** evacuate the area and escalate. There is no prize
  for handling it yourself.
- **Used oil** (compressor oil from dismantling, vacuum-pump oil from refrigerant service) is
  **California hazardous waste at any quantity.** Labeled **"Used Oil"** containers with the
  accumulation start date, kept closed, on secondary containment. Registered hauler only; receipts
  go to the EH&S Lead the same day.
- **Universal waste** — lamps, batteries, aerosol cans, e-waste — labeled with a start date, never
  held past one year.
- **Nothing goes into a floor drain, storm drain, or sanitary sewer.** Not oil, not glycol, not
  cleaning chemicals, not line-cleaning rinse, not "it's mostly water." Alameda's storm drains reach
  the Bay and this is a serious violation with personal liability. **If you're not sure, it doesn't
  go down — ask.**

### G5. Near-misses and hazard reporting

**A near-miss is a free lesson.** The pallet that almost fell teaches exactly what the one that did
teaches, without the ambulance.

Report near-misses and hazards to any supervisor, the EH&S Lead, or through the compliance app.
Anonymous is fine. **Nobody has ever been blamed for a near-miss report and nobody ever will be** —
if that ever changes, the program is broken and you should tell Sky directly.

We investigate, we fix, and we tell you what we did.

### G6. Earthquake, fire, and power outage

- **Earthquake — during shaking: drop, cover, hold on.** Away from racking, stacked product, and
  cylinder storage; the risk here is falling objects, not the ground.
- **After shaking stops:** evacuate to the assembly areas and get counted. **Do not walk the
  warehouse to "check things" first.** After any significant quake, the CO₂ tank and cylinder racks
  get inspected by the Emergency Coordinator before anyone works near them. Tank alarm or hissing =
  §B10/§E3 evacuation, upwind.
- **On the road during a quake:** pull over away from overpasses, poles, and buildings; stay in the
  vehicle until shaking stops.
- **Fire:** alarm first, evacuate, assembly area. Fight it only within §B8 limits.
- **Power outage:** emergency lighting leads to exits. Forklift and fill operations stop until power
  and the CO₂ monitor are restored. Don't work in a dark cellar or walk-in on a phone flashlight.

### G7. Regulators and inspectors

If a regulator or inspector arrives — Cal/OSHA, CUPA, health department, fire marshal:

- **Page the designated escort** (front office). Be polite and cooperative.
- **Don't answer agency questions on behalf of the company.** That's not secrecy, it's accuracy —
  you may not have the full picture and a guess becomes a finding. *"Let me get the person who
  handles that"* is a complete and correct answer.
- **Never conceal, move, or alter anything** during an inspection.
- You have the right to speak with an inspector privately about safety concerns, and to have an
  employee representative present. We will never retaliate for that.
- Full procedure lives in **SOP-10 · CUPA & Health Inspection Readiness**.
